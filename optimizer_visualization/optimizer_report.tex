\section{Overview and Shared Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The package \texttt{geko\_bayesopt} provides eight optimizer kinds, all
implemented in \path{src/geko_bayesopt/optimizer.py}: four standalone
strategies (Bayesian Optimization, Nelder--Mead, finite-difference gradient
descent, Particle Swarm Optimization) and four two-phase hybrids that combine
Bayesian Optimization with one of the heuristic methods in either order.
This section documents what all of them share; Section~\ref{sec:optimizers}
documents each kind individually, and Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} documents
the synthetic benchmark used to validate them.
Sections~\ref{sec:cfd}--\ref{sec:cfd-5d} then compare the optimizers on the
real CFD objective, with one, two, and finally five free GEKO coefficients,
and Section~\ref{sec:outlook} collects the open items.

\subsection{The ask/tell interface}

Every optimizer implements the same four-method protocol:

\begin{lstlisting}
x = opt.ask()          # suggest the next parameter vector
opt.tell(x, y)         # report the objective value y observed at x
opt.should_stop()      # True when the epsilon convergence check fires
opt.test_config()      # raises ValueError on an invalid configuration
\end{lstlisting}

The experiment loop strictly alternates
$\texttt{ask()} \rightarrow \text{evaluate} \rightarrow \texttt{tell()}$;
none of the optimizers support batched or parallel asks.
\texttt{build\_optimizer(section, parameters)} takes the \texttt{"optimizer"}
block of the experiment JSON as the \emph{section} argument
(Section~\ref{sec:config-shape}) together with the \texttt{"parameters"}
block, constructs the requested kind, and calls \texttt{test\_config()}
before returning, so invalid configurations fail at build time rather than
mid-run.

Interrupted experiments are resumed by
\emph{replaying} every completed trial through \texttt{tell()} on a fresh
optimizer object; \texttt{ask()} is never called during a replay.  Each
optimizer therefore advances its internal state machine entirely inside
\texttt{tell()}, and all eight kinds resume bit-for-bit identically to an
uninterrupted run.

\subsection{Configuration shape}
\label{sec:config-shape}

All optimizers are configured inside the \texttt{"optimizer"} block of an
experiment JSON, which always has the same top-level shape:

\begin{lstlisting}
"optimizer": {
    "kind": "<optimizer_kind>",
    "stopping_criteria": {
        "n_calls": 32,
        "epsilon": 1e-4,
        "window": 3
    },
    "kind_specific_options": { ... }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\texttt{stopping\_criteria} and \texttt{kind\_specific\_options} may be
omitted entirely to use the defaults.  The valid \texttt{kind} values are
\texttt{skopt\_gp}, \texttt{nelder\_mead}, \texttt{finite\_differences},
\texttt{pso}, \texttt{hybrid\_nm\_bayes}, \texttt{hybrid\_fd\_bayes},
\texttt{hybrid\_bayes\_nm}, and \texttt{hybrid\_bayes\_fd}.

\subsection{Stopping criteria}
\label{sec:stopping}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{9cm}@{}}
\toprule
Field & Default & Description \\
\midrule
\texttt{n\_calls} & 32 & Hard cap on total objective (CFD) evaluations. The
experiment always stops here at the latest. \\
\texttt{epsilon} & \texttt{null} & Relative-improvement threshold for early
stopping. Disabled by default; must be $>0$ when set. \\
\texttt{window} & 3 & Number of trailing evaluations over which the
improvement of the best-so-far value is measured. Must be $\geq 1$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

After each trial the optimizer checks whether the search has stalled: if
the best-so-far value has not improved by at least \texttt{epsilon}
(relative) over the last \texttt{window} evaluations, the experiment stops
early.  The check is identical for every optimizer kind and counts raw
evaluations; it can fire at the earliest after $\texttt{window} + 1$
evaluations.

An appropriate stopping criterion for this task has not been found yet.
The five-coefficient runs of Section~\ref{sec:cfd-5d} show long phases of
below-threshold improvement that are then followed by a substantial
improvement, and a short window would cut these off.  As a first
intuition, we would recommend a window of about 20 evaluations in five
dimensions (lower in lower dimensions).  For finding the supposedly global
optimum, however, the BO performance in Section~\ref{sec:cfd-5d} suggests
a window greater than 100, which makes the stopping criterion superfluous
in practice.

\subsection{Boundary policy}
\label{sec:bounds}

The parameter bounds declared in the \texttt{parameters} block are handled
differently per optimizer, deliberately:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bayesian Optimization} only ever proposes points inside the
        bounds, by construction of the search space.
  \item \textbf{Nelder--Mead and finite differences}:
        a reflection, expansion, probe, or gradient step is evaluated exactly
        where proposed, even outside the nominal range.  A poor score there
        steers the search back naturally.
  \item \textbf{PSO absorbs}: a particle that overshoots a bound is placed
        exactly on it and its velocity component in that dimension is zeroed.
\end{itemize}

With NM or FD the search can therefore leave the declared bounds; the only
hard limit is the point at which Fluent no longer accepts the proposed
coefficient values.

\subsection{Determinism}
\label{sec:determinism}

All optimizers are bitwise reproducible given the same configuration: every
stochastic component (the Sobol' initial samples, the GP
hyperparameter-fit restarts, PSO's velocity draws) is seeded from the
configuration's \texttt{random\_state}.  In addition, all five GP
construction sites (standalone \texttt{skopt\_gp} and the four hybrids)
build the identical estimator (Mat\'ern $\nu=2.5$ kernel, 10 restarts of
the kernel hyperparameter fit), so every optimizer's BO component behaves
the same and identical configurations produce identical trajectories across
runs and resumes.

\section{Optimizer Reference}
\label{sec:optimizers}

Each subsection gives the algorithm summary, a baseline configuration block
(directly usable in an experiment JSON), the meaning and valid range of every
option, and the issues one can run into.  Two recurring options are kept out
of the per-optimizer tables.  \texttt{random\_state} (default 42 for every
optimizer) seeds all stochastic components; its value matters only for
reproducibility and for comparing optimizers on identical initial samples
(Section~\ref{sec:determinism}), not for optimization quality.  The number
of Sobol' samples that precede the first GP proposal (\texttt{n\_initial}
for \texttt{skopt\_gp}, \texttt{n\_initial\_sobol} for the BO-first hybrids)
should be roughly $10\,d$ for a $d$-dimensional problem, budget permitting.
Beyond that, every optimizer has method-specific hyperparameters that can
be tuned to enhance its performance; tuning them was not a focus of this
work, but we are aware that it can be crucial
(Section~\ref{sec:outlook}).

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\texttt{skopt\_gp}: Bayesian Optimization (GP surrogate)}
\label{sec:opt-bo}

Bayesian Optimization from \texttt{scikit-optimize}: a Gaussian-process
surrogate of the objective (Mat\'ern $\nu = 2.5$ kernel), with Expected
Improvement as the acquisition function selecting the next evaluation
point.  The run starts with \texttt{n\_initial} Sobol' quasi-random
samples, then switches to model-guided proposals.

\begin{lstlisting}
"optimizer": {
    "kind": "skopt_gp",
    "stopping_criteria": { "n_calls": 20, "epsilon": 1e-4, "window": 3 },
    "kind_specific_options": {
        "n_initial": 8,
        "random_state": 42
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Possible issues.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item If \texttt{n\_initial} is close to \texttt{n\_calls}, the run is
        effectively quasi-random sampling with no model-guided phase.
  \item With a very small \texttt{n\_initial} the first GP fits rest on very
        few points and early model-guided proposals can be poor; the run
        still recovers, but wastes budget.
\end{itemize}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\texttt{nelder\_mead}: Nelder--Mead simplex}
\label{sec:opt-nm}

A derivative-free simplex method.  Phase 1 evaluates the $D{+}1$ startup simplex points one at a
time; afterwards every ask/tell cycle performs one simplex operation
(reflect, expand, contract, or one shrink step).  The startup simplex is
built around the canonical GEKO defaults
($\csep{=}1.75$, $\cnw{=}0.5$, $\cmix{=}0.0$, $\cjet{=}1.0$,
$\ccorner{=}1.0$, $\cturb{=}2.0$; only the coefficients actually being
tuned are used) with a uniform offset of $0.1$ per dimension.  No
\texttt{n\_initial} is needed.  The four coefficients are not tuned;
$\alpha$ and $\gamma$ are chosen conservatively compared to the textbook
defaults of $\alpha = 1.0$ and $\gamma = 2.0$, while $\rho$ and $\sigma$
keep the textbook values.

\begin{lstlisting}
"optimizer": {
    "kind": "nelder_mead",
    "stopping_criteria": { "n_calls": 20, "epsilon": 1e-5, "window": 3 },
    "kind_specific_options": {
        "alpha": 0.8,
        "gamma": 1.5,
        "rho": 0.5,
        "sigma": 0.5
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{8.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
Option & Default & Description \\
\midrule
\texttt{alpha} & 0.8 & Reflection coefficient. Must be $>0$. \\
\texttt{gamma} & 1.5 & Expansion coefficient. Must be $>1$. \\
\texttt{rho} & 0.5 & Contraction coefficient. Must be in $(0,1)$. \\
\texttt{sigma} & 0.5 & Shrink coefficient. Must be in $(0,1)$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Possible issues.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Local method:} Stopping criterion necessary.
  \item \textbf{Out-of-bounds proposals possible.}
\end{itemize}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{\texttt{finite\_differences}: forward-difference gradient descent}
\label{sec:opt-fd}

Classic gradient descent with a numerically estimated gradient.  Starting
from the GEKO defaults, each cycle costs $D{+}1$ evaluations: $D$ forward
probes ($\text{base} + \delta_i e_i$, one per dimension) to estimate the
gradient, then one descent step $\text{base} - \eta \nabla f$. The probe
magnitude is
$\delta_i = \mathrm{clip}\bigl((\mathrm{high}_i - \mathrm{low}_i)\cdot
\texttt{step\_size},\ \texttt{min\_step},\ \texttt{max\_step}\bigr)$.

\begin{lstlisting}
"optimizer": {
    "kind": "finite_differences",
    "stopping_criteria": { "n_calls": 20, "epsilon": 1e-4, "window": 3 },
    "kind_specific_options": {
        "step_size": 0.05,
        "learning_rate": 0.2,
        "min_step": 1e-5,
        "max_step": 0.25
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{8.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
Option & Default & Description \\
\midrule
\texttt{step\_size} & 0.05 & Probe perturbation as a fraction of each
parameter's range. Must be $>0$. \\
\texttt{learning\_rate} & 0.2 & Gradient-descent step size $\eta$. Must be
$>0$.  See the calibration note below. \\
\texttt{min\_step} & $10^{-5}$ & Minimum absolute probe perturbation. Must
be $>0$. \\
\texttt{max\_step} & 0.25 & Maximum absolute probe perturbation. Must be
$\geq$ \texttt{min\_step}. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Possible issues.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Learning-rate calibration is the make-or-break setting.}
        There is no affordable automatic fallback: a line search such as backtracking would spend several
        extra CFD evaluations per descent step.
  \item \textbf{Local method:} Stopping criterion necessary.
  \item \textbf{Probes consume budget:} of $N$ evaluations only about
        $N/(D{+}1)$ are actual descent steps; the rest are internal gradient
        probes.  Keep this in mind when picking the iteration budget:
        otherwise the run ends having approximated a gradient it never
        uses.
  \item \textbf{Out-of-bounds proposals possible.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\texttt{pso}: Particle Swarm Optimization}
\label{sec:opt-pso}

\texttt{n\_particles} particles move under the standard
velocity update
\[
v \leftarrow w\,v
  + c_1 r_1 \,(\mathbf{x}_{pbest} - \mathbf{x})
  + c_2 r_2 \,(\mathbf{x}_{gbest} - \mathbf{x}),
\qquad r_1, r_2 \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1),
\]
with the inertia weight $w$ decaying linearly from $w_{start}$ to $w_{end}$
over the run.  The swarm hyperparameters are not tuned; they follow common
textbook values: the linearly decaying inertia weight $0.9 \to 0.4$ is the
classic schedule, and $c_1 = c_2 = 1.5$ sits slightly below the classic
$2.0$, a slightly more conservative attraction.  Initial positions are drawn from a Sobol' sequence (seeded the
same way as the BO optimizers, so PSO and BO runs with equal
\texttt{random\_state} are comparable); initial velocities are zero.
Velocity is clamped component-wise to $\pm v_{max}$
($= \texttt{v\_max\_frac} \times$ range); bounds are handled by absorption
(Section~\ref{sec:bounds}).

The number of swarm iterations is \emph{derived from the budget}, not
configured.  The initial Sobol' sweep and every swarm iteration each cost
one evaluation per particle, so the budget must satisfy
$\texttt{n\_calls} = \texttt{n\_particles} \times (1 + \texttt{max\_iter})$,
giving $\texttt{max\_iter} = \texttt{n\_calls} / \texttt{n\_particles} - 1$
(the $-1$ is the init sweep); the inertia decay exactly spans those
iterations.

\begin{lstlisting}
"optimizer": {
    "kind": "pso",
    "stopping_criteria": { "n_calls": 200, "epsilon": 1e-4, "window": 5 },
    "kind_specific_options": {
        "n_particles": 10,
        "w_start": 0.9,
        "w_end": 0.4,
        "c1": 1.5,
        "c2": 1.5,
        "v_max_frac": 0.2,
        "random_state": 42
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{8.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
Option & Default & Description \\
\midrule
\texttt{n\_particles} & 10 & Swarm size. Must be $\geq 2$. \\
\texttt{w\_start} & 0.9 & Initial inertia weight. Requires
$0 < w_{end} \leq w_{start} \leq 1$. \\
\texttt{w\_end} & 0.4 & Final inertia weight (reached at
\texttt{max\_iter}). \\
\texttt{c1} & 1.5 & Cognitive coefficient (pull toward personal best).
Must be $>0$. \\
\texttt{c2} & 1.5 & Social coefficient (pull toward global best).
Must be $>0$. \\
\texttt{v\_max\_frac} & 0.2 & Velocity cap per dimension as a fraction of
that dimension's range, in $(0,1]$.  Applied every iteration, not just at
initialization. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Possible issues.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Budget divisibility:} \texttt{test\_config()} raises unless
        \texttt{n\_calls} is divisible by \texttt{n\_particles} (so the
        budget divides into whole swarm iterations) and
        $\texttt{n\_calls} \geq 2 \times \texttt{n\_particles}$ (init sweep
        plus at least one swarm iteration).
  \item \textbf{Particles vs.\ iterations trade-off:} with a fixed budget,
        more particles means broader coverage per iteration but fewer
        iterations to converge.  The real-CFD sweep
        (Section~\ref{sec:cfd-pso}) found small swarms (3--5 particles)
        preferable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\texttt{hybrid\_nm\_bayes}: Nelder--Mead $\to$ Bayesian}
\label{sec:opt-nmbo}

Warm-start hybrid.  Phase 1: the first \texttt{n\_initial} evaluations run
Nelder--Mead (self-initialized from the GEKO defaults, exactly as in
Section~\ref{sec:opt-nm}).  Phase 2: the remaining
$\texttt{n\_calls} - \texttt{n\_initial}$ evaluations run a GP that was
warm-started with the NM history (the BO optimizer is constructed with zero
initial points of its own and receives every in-bounds NM result via
\texttt{tell()}).

\begin{lstlisting}
"optimizer": {
    "kind": "hybrid_nm_bayes",
    "stopping_criteria": { "n_calls": 22, "epsilon": 1e-4, "window": 3 },
    "kind_specific_options": {
        "n_initial": 10,
        "bo_options": { "bayesian_kind": "GP", "random_state": 42 },
        "nm_options": { "alpha": 0.8, "gamma": 1.5, "rho": 0.5, "sigma": 0.5 }
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{8.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
Option & Default & Description \\
\midrule
\texttt{n\_initial} & 8 & Number of NM evaluations before the switch.
Must be $\geq 1$. \\
\texttt{bo\_options.bayesian\_kind} & \texttt{"GP"} & Phase-2 surrogate:
\texttt{"GP"}, \texttt{"RF"}, \texttt{"ET"}, or \texttt{"GBRT"}.
\texttt{"GP"} resolves to the same explicitly built estimator as
\texttt{skopt\_gp}. \\
\texttt{nm\_options} & (see \ref{sec:opt-nm}) & Same options as standalone
\texttt{nelder\_mead}. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Possible issues.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Out-of-bounds warm-up points are dropped from the BO warm start by
        construction. Edge case.
  \item Inherited local bias.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\texttt{hybrid\_fd\_bayes}: Finite Differences $\to$ Bayesian}
\label{sec:opt-fdbo}

Warm-start hybrid, identical in structure to \texttt{hybrid\_nm\_bayes} but
with finite-difference gradient descent as the Phase-1 method.

\begin{lstlisting}
"optimizer": {
    "kind": "hybrid_fd_bayes",
    "stopping_criteria": { "n_calls": 60, "epsilon": 1e-4, "window": 3 },
    "kind_specific_options": {
        "n_initial": 20,
        "bo_options": { "bayesian_kind": "GP", "random_state": 42 },
        "fd_options": {
            "step_size": 0.05, "learning_rate": 0.2,
            "min_step": 1e-5, "max_step": 0.25
        }
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\texttt{bo\_options} as in Section~\ref{sec:opt-nmbo};
\texttt{fd\_options} takes the same options as standalone
\texttt{finite\_differences}; \texttt{n\_initial} (default 8, $\geq 1$) is
the number of FD evaluations before the switch.

\paragraph{Possible issues.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item All issues of standalone FD apply during Phase 1 (learning-rate
        calibration, probes consuming budget).
  \item Out-of-bounds warm-up points are dropped from the BO warm start by
        construction. Edge case.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\texttt{hybrid\_bayes\_nm}: Bayesian $\to$ Nelder--Mead}
\label{sec:opt-bonm}

Refinement hybrid.  Phase 1: the first \texttt{n\_initial} evaluations run
BO (Sobol' sampling for \texttt{n\_initial\_sobol} points, then GP
proposals).  Phase 2: Nelder--Mead refinement, with its startup simplex
re-centred on the best point found by BO (instead of the GEKO defaults),
using the same uniform $0.1$ offsets scaled by \texttt{simplex\_scale}.

\begin{lstlisting}
"optimizer": {
    "kind": "hybrid_bayes_nm",
    "stopping_criteria": { "n_calls": 30, "epsilon": 1e-4, "window": 3 },
    "kind_specific_options": {
        "n_initial": 10,
        "bo_options": {
            "n_initial_sobol": 5, "bayesian_kind": "GP", "random_state": 42
        },
        "nm_options": {
            "alpha": 0.8, "gamma": 1.5, "rho": 0.5, "sigma": 0.5,
            "simplex_scale": 0.6
        }
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{7.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
Option & Default & Description \\
\midrule
\texttt{n\_initial} & 8 & Number of BO evaluations before the switch.
Must be $\geq 1$. \\
\texttt{bo\_options.n\_initial\_sobol} & $\min(5, \texttt{n\_initial})$ &
Sobol' samples within the BO phase before the GP takes over. \\
\texttt{bo\_options.bayesian\_kind} & \texttt{"GP"} & As in
Section~\ref{sec:opt-nmbo}. \\
\texttt{nm\_options.simplex\_scale} & 1.0 & Scale factor for the NM startup
simplex built around the BO best point; values $<1$ tighten local
exploitation (e.g.\ $0.6$ shrinks the offsets to $\pm 0.06$).  Ignored by
the standalone \texttt{nelder\_mead} kind. \\
\texttt{nm\_options} (rest) & (see \ref{sec:opt-nm}) & Same options as
standalone \texttt{nelder\_mead}. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Possible issues.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hyperparameters do not fit the local properties.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\texttt{hybrid\_bayes\_fd}: Bayesian $\to$ Finite Differences}
\label{sec:opt-bofd}

Refinement hybrid.  Phase 1 as in \texttt{hybrid\_bayes\_nm}.  Phase 2:
finite-difference descent starting from the BO best point, which is injected
directly as the FD base \emph{without re-evaluating it}; the FD phase
begins immediately with a gradient probe rather than wasting an evaluation
on the already-known base.  Because BO has already localized a promising
region, the FD phase should run tighter than a standalone FD run; roughly
$60\%$ of the standalone \texttt{step\_size} and \texttt{learning\_rate} is
a reasonable starting point.

\begin{lstlisting}
"optimizer": {

    "kind_specific_options": {
        "n_initial": 10,
        "bo_options": {
            "n_initial_sobol": 5, "bayesian_kind": "GP", "random_state": 42
        },
        "fd_options": {
            "step_size": 0.03, "learning_rate": 0.009,
            "min_step": 1e-5, "max_step": 0.25
        }
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\texttt{bo\_options.bayesian\_kind} & \texttt{"GP"} & As in

\section{Synthetic Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

Before being applied to real CFD cases, all eight optimizers are exercised
on cheap analytic test functions. The
goal here is only to show that every optimizer works and behaves according
to its design; the quantitative comparison happens on the real objective
(Section~\ref{sec:cfd}).

\subsection{Test functions}
\label{sec:testfns}

Both test functions are deliberately \emph{bimodal}: they possess a local
and a global minimum, with the starting point placed near a local
maximum between them, a trap for purely local methods.  On
$x \in [0.5, 3.5]$:
\begin{equation}
f_{1D}(x) = -2.5x \,\sin(2.5x),
\label{eq:f1d}
\end{equation}
with the local minimum at $x \approx 0.811$ ($f \approx -1.820$), the
global minimum at $x \approx 3.191$ ($f \approx -7.917$), and the start at
$x = 1.75$ (the GEKO default of the mapped coefficient).  On
$(x_1, x_2) \in [0.5, 3.5] \times [0.1, 0.9]$:
\begin{equation}
f_{2D}(x_1, x_2) = f_{1D}(x_1) + 3\,\lvert x_2 - 0.5 \rvert.
\label{eq:f2d}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The GEDCP cost function}
\label{sec:cfd-costfn}

The optimizer minimizes a single scalar score, the \emph{General Error and
Default-Coefficient Preference} (GEDCP) score.  It combines a field-matching
error against DNS with a mild penalty for straying from GEKO's own
recommended defaults:

\begin{equation}
f(\mathbf{c}) \;=\; E_F(\mathbf{c}) \cdot \Bigl(1 + \lambda_p\, p(\mathbf{c})\Bigr),
\qquad \lambda_p = 0.5
\label{eq:gedcp}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Field error $E_F$.} A sum over five DNS-observed fields
$\Phi = \{U_x,\, U_y,\, k,\, P_k,\, \varepsilon\}$ (streamwise velocity,
wall-normal velocity, turbulent kinetic energy, production of $k$, and
turbulent dissipation rate) of the area-weighted,
DNS-standard-deviation-normalized $L_2$ error between simulation and DNS:

\begin{equation}
E_F(\mathbf{c}) \;=\; \sum_{\phi \in \Phi}
\sqrt{\; \dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i} w_i \left(\dfrac{\phi^{DNS}_i - \phi^{sim}_i(\mathbf{c})}{\sigma^{DNS}_\phi}\right)^{\!2}}
{\displaystyle\sum_i w_i} \;}
\label{eq:fielderr}
\end{equation}

where $i$ indexes DNS sample points, $w_i$ is each point's cell-area
weight (so densely sampled regions do not dominate the score), and
$\sigma^{DNS}_\phi$ is the area-weighted standard deviation of DNS field
$\phi$, which puts the five fields on comparable scales.  DNS points
inside the hill surface are excluded, and the simulation field is
interpolated onto the remaining DNS points before the error is taken.

Equation~\eqref{eq:fielderr} is the $L_2$ field-error norm, selected in the
experiment JSON as \texttt{"field\_error\_norm": "l2"} inside the
\texttt{gedcp} objective options.  Every result in this and the following
sections uses this $L_2$ objective.

\paragraph{Coefficient preference $p$.} A mean relative deviation of the
optimized coefficients from their GEKO defaults:

\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{c}) \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathbf{c}|} \sum_{j} \left| \frac{c_j^{default} - c_j}{c_j^{default}} \right|
\label{eq:pref}
\end{equation}

summed only over the coefficient(s) actually being tuned in a given study
(just $\csep$ in the 1-D study; $\csep$ and $\cnw$ in the 2-D study).  A
coefficient whose default is zero ($\cmix$ in the five-coefficient study of
Section~\ref{sec:cfd-5d}) enters with its absolute deviation instead, since
the relative deviation is undefined for a zero reference.

The integral-quantity error term is disabled ($\lambda_I = 0$) in every
configuration of this study, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:gedcp} reduces to
$f(\mathbf{c}) = E_F(\mathbf{c})\,(1 + 0.5\,p(\mathbf{c}))$ in practice.
As a reference point, the GEKO-default simulation ($\csep=1.75$,
$\cnw=0.5$, $p=0$) scores $f = 2.9683$ under this metric in both studies.

\subsection{Does the refinement phase actually help?}
\label{sec:cfd-refinement}

The BO$\to$NM and BO$\to$FD hybrids are designed so that a cheap
local-refinement phase improves on whatever BO already found.  Whether that
happened in practice can be checked directly, by asking which iteration
produced each run's reported best score, and whether that iteration fell
before or after the phase boundary:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccl@{}}
\toprule

\midrule
BO $\to$ NM & 1-D & 14 & 14 & No: best is BO's own last point \\
BO $\to$ FD & 1-D & 14 & 14 & No: best is BO's own last point \\
BO $\to$ NM & 2-D & 35 & 57 & Yes: improved until iter.\ 57/60 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

In three of the four cases tested, the refinement phase contributed no

kept improving until iteration 57 of 60, showing the refinement idea can
work but did not reliably do so here.  With only one run per configuration,
this is not evidence that refinement is useless in general.

\subsection{Choice of optimizer}
\label{sec:cfd-choice}

With the synthetic benchmark showing that every optimizer behaves according
to its design, and the 1-D and 2-D CFD studies showing that all of them
also work on the real objective, the headline result of the comparison is
that no optimizer really outperforms another. The local-vs-global trap of the synthetic benchmark does not
materialize on the real objective, whose cost surface behaves close to
unimodal over the tested ranges.  The choice of a primary optimizer
therefore rests on assumptions and robustness rather than on raw scores.

On that basis, Bayesian Optimization is a fair choice as the project's
primary optimizer.  It is a global method: the Sobol' initialization covers
the whole box, and the acquisition function keeps balancing exploration
against exploitation afterwards, so it cannot lock permanently onto a local
basin.  It requires no gradients and assumes neither convexity nor
unimodality of the objective.  Beyond continuity, the Mat\'ern $\nu=2.5$
kernel models functions that are twice differentiable in the mean-square
sense; this smoothness enters from the modeling perspective and is not
necessary for the optimizer to work: the kernel is itself a hyperparameter
and can be switched, and an objective that violates the modeled smoothness
(such as the kink of the 2-D test function) degrades the surrogate fit
locally without breaking the search (Section~\ref{sec:res-bo}).

\section{Particle Swarm Optimization on the Real Objective}
\label{sec:cfd-pso}

Particle Swarm Optimization is included alongside BO.  Strictly speaking
PSO is not a global optimizer, but its stochastic velocity components make
it unlikely to get stuck in local minima, if any exist.  Its critical
structural advantage over every other method in this study is that the
particle evaluations within a swarm iteration are independent of one
another, so PSO parallelizes trivially across concurrent Fluent sessions.
Due to this potential, a particle-count sweep of PSO is run in both
dimensions, checking whether PSO reaches optima comparable to BO/NM; the
head-to-head comparison against BO follows in the five-coefficient study
(Section~\ref{sec:cfd-5d}).  Every run is executed sequentially, one
particle at a time: exploiting PSO's parallelism is not possible in this
work, because ANSYS Fluent does not allow parallel sessions on the student
version.

\subsection{Sweep discussion}

PSO also finds the minimum: the best sweep points tie BO/NM's 1-D optimum
exactly, and the best 2-D result lands within one percent of the best 2-D
score.  Performance varies with the particle count, however: at a roughly
fixed budget, more particles leave fewer swarm iterations to converge, and
both sweeps degrade with growing particle count.  The head-to-head
comparison against BO continues in five dimensions
(Section~\ref{sec:cfd-5d}).

\subsection{Interpretation}

Both optimizers find the same optimum region, and at the shared budget
their best scores are essentially tied: BO finishes at $f = 1.9651$, the
15-particle PSO run at $f = 1.9663$, a gap of under $0.1\%$.  The
particle-count trend repeats the 1-D sweep of Section~\ref{sec:cfd-pso}:
fewer particles with more swarm iterations beat more particles with fewer
iterations ($15$ particles / $19$ iterations at $1.9663$, then $30/9$ at
$1.9880$, then $50/5$ at $1.9948$).

The BO trajectory also supplies the observation behind the stopping-window
recommendation of Section~\ref{sec:stopping}: after reaching $f = 1.990$
at around evaluation $110$, BO improves below any practical threshold for
roughly $150$ evaluations before dropping to its final $1.9651$ shortly
after evaluation $260$.  A stopping criterion with a short window would
have cut the run off well before its best point.

All four runs agree on where the optimum lies: $\cnw$ stays on its default
($\approx 0.50$, as in the 2-D study), $\cmix \approx 0.18$--$0.24$,
$\cturb \approx 0.58$--$0.71$ (well below its default of $2.0$), and
$\cjet \approx 0.88$--$1.01$.  Notably, the best $\csep$ sits at
$1.75$--$2.06$, near its GEKO default, rather than at the
$\approx 0.84$--$0.87$ that both lower-dimensional studies find: with
$\cmix$, $\cturb$, and $\cjet$ free, the preferred separation coefficient
shifts, a direct sign of coefficient interaction that a
one-coefficient-at-a-time calibration would miss.

It is fair to conclude from these runs that PSO, with suitable parameters,
can absolutely compete with BO on this objective.  Where the two differ is
wall-clock potential: PSO's budget comes in rounds of independent
evaluations ($20$ rounds of $15$ for the best run), so with concurrent
Fluent sessions its wall-clock cost would shrink by up to the particle
count, whereas BO's $300$ evaluations are strictly sequential.  Every run
here is a single seed, so the spread between the PSO variants should be
read with that caveat.

\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:outlook}

Three items remain open before the optimizer story of this project can be
considered closed:

\paragraph{PSO hyperparameter tuning.}  None of PSO's hyperparameters
(inertia schedule, cognitive/social coefficients, velocity cap, particle
count) were systematically tuned for the periodic-hills objective.  The
values of Section~\ref{sec:cfd-pso-config} were chosen empirically:
starting from textbook values, they were adjusted once after the 2-D study
toward a slightly more conservative, less explorative setting (weaker
attraction coefficients, halved velocity cap, lower final inertia weight)
and have not been touched since.  Given how directly the particle-count
sweeps show configuration affecting the reached optimum, a systematic
hyperparameter search for PSO is the most promising next step; see
\cite{pso_future_work_ref} for guidance on hyperparameter selection.

\paragraph{PSO parallelization.}  PSO's particles are independent within a
swarm iteration, and Section~\ref{sec:cfd-5d} shows this parallelism is
what makes its larger budgets affordable in wall-clock terms.  Every run in
this document is nevertheless executed one particle at a time, precisely
because ANSYS Fluent's student version does not allow parallel sessions.
Implementing concurrent particle evaluation (dispatching all particles of a
swarm iteration to separate Fluent sessions at once) therefore remains open
and requires a full license; it is the prerequisite for realizing the
wall-clock advantage in practice; see \cite{pso_future_work_ref}.

\paragraph{BO hyperparameter verification.}  BO's kernel and estimator
settings (Mat\'ern $\nu=2.5$, 10 kernel-fit restarts, Sobol' initialization)
are adopted from \cite{bo_hyperparams_ref}, a study carried out in two
dimensions.  Whether these settings remain optimal for the five-coefficient
space (and the larger spaces this project is heading toward) has not been
verified and should be double-checked before BO's results are treated as
the ceiling of what the method can deliver.
